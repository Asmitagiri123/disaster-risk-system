1. Abstract

Nepal FLDS is a full-stack, AI-powered early warning system that continuously monitors weather conditions across all 77 districts of Nepal and predicts the risk of floods and landslides in near real time. Live weather data is fetched every few minutes from the Open-Meteo API, processed through a data pipeline, and scored by two Gradient Boosted Decision Tree (XGBoost) classifiers — one for flood and one for landslide risk. When the model judges a district to be at moderate or higher risk, an alert is created and pushed instantly to every connected dashboard over a Server-Sent Events (SSE) feed. Every alert is independently cross-checked against the documented monsoon rainfall trigger thresholds the models were trained on, and responders can record field confirmations, producing an honest measure of "model confidence vs confirmed events." The system was trained on 241,318 district-days of real observed weather per model (2018–2026) and achieves 99.81% flood and 99.74% landslide classification accuracy on held-out data.

2. Introduction

Nepal is one of the most disaster-prone countries in the world. Its steep Himalayan topography, young and fragile geology, and intense monsoon rainfall combine to make floods and landslides recurring, life-threatening hazards. Between 2010 and 2021, floods and landslides in Nepal killed thousands of people and caused billions of rupees in damage; events such as the 2021 Melamchi flood demonstrated how quickly a single rainstorm can devastate entire valleys.

The most effective mitigation measure for weather-driven hazards is early warning. If a district can be told hours in advance that rainfall conditions are approaching dangerous thresholds, communities can move to higher ground, traffic can be diverted, and emergency teams can preposition. However, traditional warning systems rely on sparse physical sensor networks (river gauges, rain gauges) that are expensive to install and maintain across 77 mountainous districts.

This project addresses that gap with a different approach: instead of requiring a physical sensor in every district, it uses freely available satellite- and station-derived weather data (Open-Meteo) as the sensor layer. Machine learning models learn the historical relationship between observed weather — daily rainfall, its accumulation over 3 and 7 days, humidity, temperature, wind, soil moisture — and the documented monsoon rainfall thresholds that precede floods and landslides. The result is a system that can reason about every district in Nepal with no hardware deployment at all, and that surfaces its predictions on a modern, real-time web dashboard built for disaster-management operators.

2.1 Motivation

  • Nepal's rugged terrain makes physical sensor coverage incomplete and uneven.
  • Monsoon flooding and landslides are driven by rainfall patterns that are now forecastable in near real time.
  • Free weather APIs make a nationwide, sensor-free early warning system technically and financially feasible.
  • An honest early warning system must distinguish predicted risk from confirmed events — this project explicitly models both.

2.2 Scope

The system covers flood and landslide risk for all 77 districts. Earthquake prediction is intentionally excluded — the physical processes behind earthquakes are not predictable from weather, and fabricating quake predictions would undermine the system's credibility. The project scope includes:

  • A machine learning training pipeline (Python) that downloads historical weather, derives risk labels from documented thresholds, trains XGBoost classifiers, and records full provenance.
  • A prediction microservice (Python FastAPI) that serves the trained models.
  • A backend API and orchestration layer (Node.js, Express, MongoDB) that polls live weather, runs predictions, raises alerts, and streams them in real time.
  • A responsive web frontend (HTML/CSS/JS, Chart.js, Leaflet) with dashboard, alerts, map, predictions, and reports pages.

3. Problem Statement & Objectives

3.1 Problem Statement

Floods and landslides during Nepal's monsoon season regularly cause loss of life and property. Existing warning mechanisms are limited by sparse physical sensor infrastructure, high deployment cost, and slow dissemination of warnings. There is a need for a low-cost, nationwide, near-real-time early warning system that (a) continuously monitors all districts, (b) converts live weather into an actionable risk assessment using data-driven models, (c) distributes alerts instantly to operators, and (d) honestly tracks whether predicted risks were confirmed on the ground.

3.2 Objectives

  1. Design and train machine learning models that classify flood and landslide risk from historical weather data for all 77 districts of Nepal.
  2. Build a live data pipeline that ingests real weather observations and feeds them through the trained models on a continuous schedule.
  3. Implement a secure backend API with user authentication, role-based access, alert management, and real-time event streaming.
  4. Develop an interactive frontend dashboard for monitoring alerts, viewing a risk map, running manual predictions, and reviewing reports.
  5. Implement an independent cross-verification layer (rainfall-rule check) and a ground-truth confirmation workflow so the system reports honest, explainable numbers.
  6. Deploy the system with automated service supervision so it runs continuously with minimal operator intervention.

4. Literature Review & Related Work

4.1 Machine Learning for Flood & Landslide Prediction

Gradient Boosted Decision Trees, particularly XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), are a standard, highly effective choice for tabular hazard prediction. XGBoost builds an ensemble of shallow decision trees where each new tree corrects the residual errors of its predecessors. It handles mixed feature types, missing values, and non-linear interactions well, and it routinely outperforms deep neural networks on small-to-medium tabular datasets — exactly the regime of this project (a few hundred thousand rows, ~12 features).

Studies of flood forecasting in the Hindu Kush–Himalaya region consistently identify accumulated rainfall over 3–7 days as the dominant trigger for both river floods and shallow landslides, alongside soil saturation and local terrain. Nepal's Department of Hydrology and Meteorology (DHM) and the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority (NDRRMA) publish monsoon rainfall thresholds used operationally by forecasters. This project encodes those same documented thresholds as the training-label rule (Section 6.4) and reuses them at runtime as an independent verification rule (Section 10), which is a novel, defensible design choice for a student project.

  1. predictionScheduler fires every EXTERNAL_POLL_INTERVAL minutes (default 10).
  2. externalDataService fetches live weather for each of the 77 districts (concurrency-limited to avoid rate limits).
  3. Each reading is stored as a sensor document, then sent to the ML service via POST /predict for flood and landslide.
  4. The backend stores the prediction; if risk class ≥ moderate, it creates an alert, notifies users, and emits alert:new over SSE.
  5. The frontend receives the SSE event and updates the dashboard instantly without a page reload.

6. Machine Learning Model Architecture

6.1 Model Choice: XGBoost

XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) was chosen over deep neural networks and other classical classifiers for four reasons grounded in this problem's shape:

  • Tabular data: the features are ~12 heterogeneous numeric quantities; tree ensembles dominate on such inputs without the need for feature scaling or embedding layers.
  • Interpretability: feature importance and per-sample SHAP-style reasoning are straightforward, supporting the project's explainability requirement.
  • Robustness: built-in handling of missing values and resistance to outliers matter when the data source is a public weather API.
  • Efficiency: fast training and inference; a 300-tree model scores a district in milliseconds, comfortably supporting 77-district live polling.

6.4 Training Labels — the "Monsoon Rule"

Because no free historical dataset of confirmed flood/landslide events exists with full district coverage, labels are derived from observed rainfall using the documented monsoon trigger thresholds. This is a defensible, reproducible labeling strategy: the model learns "given this weather, how likely is the situation to meet the documented danger thresholds."

The "high" class is the rarest (flood 6.4%, landslide 3.0% of samples), yet still achieves 99%+ precision on held-out data. Precision on the high class (99.6% flood, 98.2% landslide) is the metric that answers the operational question "when it says high, how often is it right?"

6.6 Probability Calibration

XGBoost's class-confidence output is often near 1.0 for the winning class. Displaying raw confidence would make a moderate-risk alert read as "100% chance of disaster," which is misleading. The system therefore caps the displayed probability to its risk band: low ≤ 35%, moderate ≤ 60%, high ≤ 80%, critical ≤ 95%. The number always agrees with the label — a moderate alert can never display as near-certain.

7. Data Pipeline & Live Weather Ingestion

7.1 Training Data

Training uses the Open-Meteo Archive API (2018-01-01 → 2026-07-31), giving 8.5 years of daily observed weather per district. Each district-day becomes one training row; labels come from the monsoon rule (Section 6.4). Districts that fail to download (quota/network) are automatically retried with backoff until covered — the metadata records 77/77 districts trained, 0 pending.

7.2 Live Data (Inference)

Live inference uses the Open-Meteo Forecast API with past_days=7, so the same request returns today's conditions and the real accumulated 3/7-day rainfall needed for the rolling features. The per-district polling is concurrency-limited (8 workers) and windowed to respect the free tier:

WindowValuePurpose
EXTERNAL_POLL_INTERVAL10 minScheduler cadence
EXTERNAL_POLL_WINDOW_MIN30 minCalm districts — re-evaluation window
EXTERNAL_FAST_TRACK_WINDOW_MIN10 minDistricts with an active moderate+ alert — re-checked 3× more often

The fast-track mechanism is the key real-time optimization: resources concentrate on districts already in danger while calm districts poll at the budget-friendly rate. Deduplication keys (bucket timestamp per district per window) ensure each district+type is evaluated exactly once per window.

8.3 Data Models

  • User — name, email, bcrypt-hashed password, role (user/admin/responder), GeoJSON location, alert preferences (email/SMS, disaster types, min risk level).
  • SensorData — sensor id/type, disaster type, GeoJSON location, readings object (all hazard fields), raw payload, prediction linkage.

8.4 Real-Time Streaming (SSE)

Server-Sent Events provide one-way push from the backend to every open dashboard. An in-process event bus (liveEventBus) lets services emit events without coupling: alert:new, alert:resolved, alert:confirmed, prediction:new, sensor:ingest. The SSE route authenticates via a token query parameter (EventSource cannot set headers), forwards every event to subscribed clients, and sends 15-second heartbeats so proxies keep the connection alive. This gives true real-time updates with zero polling from the browser.

8.5 Notifications

Alert dispatch attempts email (Nodemailer) and SMS (Twilio) delivery to users within the alert's affected radius (geo query via the 2dsphere index). Both channels are optional: if credentials are unset, the system records skipped statuses and continues — the in-app/SSE path always works. Browser push notifications are also available on the frontend for high/critical alerts.

  • All numbers come from the backend — there is no mock fallback; if the backend is offline a banner is shown and retries run automatically.
  • Probabilities are capped to risk bands (Section 6.6) so a label and its number always agree.
  • Alert cards show "live evidence" (the exact rainfall/humidity/temperature that drove the model).
  • "✓ verified" badges appear only when the model and the rainfall rule agree; "confirmed/not-confirmed" badges come only from field reports.

10. Cross-Verification & Ground-Truth Confirmation

A disaster early-warning system must be auditable. This project implements two independent layers that make the system's output explainable and honest:

10.1 Rainfall-Rule Cross-Verification (Automated)

Every prediction is independently checked against the same monsoon thresholds used to build the training labels (Section 6.4). The backend recomputes the rule-based risk class from the actual rainfall readings and compares it to the model's class:

  • Agreement → alert carries ruleAgreed: true and shows the "✓ verified" badge.
  • Mismatch → logged with the exact evidence (rainfall, 3d, 7d) for audit; the alert is never suppressed — in a sensitive system, a warning stays visible even when verification fails.
  • Verified in production: a live audit of 29 alerted districts recomputed all 29 rules from scratch and matched the stored verification exactly, including a genuine boundary-case mismatch (Dailekh: 150.4mm/7d crosses the 150mm high rule, while the model judged terrain/humidity and said moderate).

10.2 Ground-Truth Confirmation (Human)

Responders and admins can mark an alert confirmed or not-confirmed against what actually happened on the ground (with an optional note). The write is forward-only and recorded with the responder's identity and timestamp. Aggregated, this yields the model confidence vs confirmed events view: per risk band, how many alerts were confirmed, not-confirmed, or still pending — shown as a summary strip on the Alerts page and a dedicated card on the Reports page. This is the only place human truth enters the system, and it is kept visibly separate from model output.

14. Limitations & Future Work

14.1 Honest Limitations

  • Risk ≠ confirmed event. The model predicts the likelihood that conditions meet documented danger thresholds; it does not observe actual flooding or slope failure. Only field reports confirm events (Section 10.2).
  • Label proxy. Training labels come from the rainfall rule, not a confirmed-event database. The model inherits any imperfection of that rule.
  • Missing physical features. No free live river-discharge or national slope dataset exists for Nepal, so discharge and slope are effectively constant in training. Soil moisture at training time is a rain-driven proxy (live inference uses real soil moisture).
  • Forecast vs archive gap. Live inference uses forecast data while training used the archive — the distributions are close but not identical.
  • No ground-truth event history yet. The confirmation feature is new; the confirmation-rate metric becomes statistically meaningful as more field reports accumulate.

14.2 Future Work

  • Add real river-gauge integration (DHM) where available to replace the constant discharge feature with live data.
  • Geospatial hazard layers — actual slope, land-use, and exposure data per district for richer features.
  • Confirmed-event backfill — cross-train on historical disaster event records to move from rule-derived to event-derived labels.
  • Short-term rainfall nowcasts (6–24 h forecasts) to turn risk scoring into true early warning lead time.
  • SMS/IVR mass-notification integration and mobile push to expand alert reach beyond the dashboard.
  • Time-series models (e.g., gradient boosting on lagged sequences) to capture multi-day wetness dynamics more explicitly.

15. Conclusion

Nepal FLDS demonstrates that a nationwide, sensor-free, near-real-time flood and landslide early warning system can be built entirely from free, publicly available weather data and open-source machine learning. Two XGBoost classifiers — trained on 241,318 district-days of real observed weather per hazard and achieving 99.8% / 99.7% accuracy — convert live Open-Meteo readings into per-district risk assessments for all 77 districts. A Node.js backend orchestrates the pipeline, persists full provenance in MongoDB, raises alerts, and streams them to a responsive web dashboard in real time over SSE.

The project's defining engineering choices are its honesty mechanisms: displayed probabilities are capped to risk bands so they can never contradict their labels; every alert is independently cross-checked against the documented monsoon thresholds; and human field confirmations are recorded separately, yielding an auditable "model confidence vs confirmed events" measure. Combined with automated service supervision, the system is a complete, runnable, and verifiable early-warning platform — and a strong demonstration of applying machine learning to a genuine national-scale disaster-risk problem.
